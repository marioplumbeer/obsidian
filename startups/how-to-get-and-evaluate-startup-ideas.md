# How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas (YC Startup School)

## Summary
Jared Friedman, a Y Combinator Group Partner, distills how the top 100 YC companies by valuation actually found their ideas, drawing on that quantitative analysis, [Paul Graham's essay "How to Get Startup Ideas"](https://www.paulgraham.com/startupideas.html), and years of watching founders pivot mid-batch. Core message: don't hunt for a "good startup idea" in the abstract — fall in love with a real problem, pick a fertile idea space that fits your team, and treat your first idea as a starting point that will morph rather than a fixed bet.

Source: [How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas | Startup School](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Th8JoIan4dg)

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. The four most common startup-idea mistakes
The most common mistake is building a "solution in search of a problem" (a CISP) — starting from a technology ("AI is cool, what could I apply it to?") instead of a real problem people already care about. The fix is to fall in love with a high-quality, *specific* problem, not an abstract societal one like global poverty.

The second mistake is getting stuck on **tar pit ideas** — common, superficially plausible ideas (the classic example is "an app to coordinate weekend plans with friends") that have quietly defeated founders for 20 years because of a hidden structural reason. They're not impossible, just much harder than they look; Friedman's advice is to Google the idea first and talk to people who already tried and failed.

The third and fourth mistakes sit at opposite ends of a spectrum: jumping on the first idea without testing it, versus waiting indefinitely for the "perfect" idea (which doesn't exist). Paul Graham's framing: treat your idea as a good **starting point** that will morph anyway.

### 2. Ten questions to test whether a startup idea is good
Friedman's framework for evaluating an idea you already have:

1. **Founder-market fit** — are you the right team for this idea? [PlanGrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlanGrid) (construction blueprints on iPad) is the model example: co-founder Tracy Young had worked in construction, co-founder Ralph Gootee was a strong engineer. Friedman reframes the whole exercise: don't look for a good idea in the abstract, look for a good idea *for your team*.
2. **How big is the market?** — either big now, or small and rapidly growing. [Coinbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinbase) started in 2012 when the Bitcoin trading market was tiny but obviously headed for billions.
3. **How acute is the problem?** — [Brex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brex) (Winter 2017) is the example: before Brex, a startup literally could not get a corporate credit card. If the alternative to your product is nothing, that's a good problem.
4. **Do you have competition?** — counter-intuitively, good. Most great startup ideas have competitors; going up against entrenched competition just means you need a real new insight.
5. **Do you personally want this, and do you know people who do?**
6. **Did this only recently become possible or necessary?** — [Checkr](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/checkr) (background checks via API) exists because gig-economy platforms like DoorDash and Instacart suddenly needed to background-check huge pools of workers, a use case older background-check companies weren't built for.
7. **Do you have a good proxy?** — a large company doing something similar in a different market. [Rappi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rappi) used DoorDash's success in the US as a proxy that food delivery would work in Latin America.
8. **Would you want to work on this for years?** — a nice-to-have, not required; passion often grows *with* a working business rather than preceding it (tax/accounting software is Friedman's example of a "boring" business people grow to love).
9. **Is this a scalable business?** — pure software scales; services businesses built on high-skill human labor are where founders get into trouble.
10. **Is this a good idea space?** — an idea space is one level of abstraction above a single idea (e.g. "software for hospitals"). Idea spaces have wildly different historical hit rates — fintech infrastructure and vertical SaaS for enterprise did far better over the last decade than consumer hardware or ad tech. [Fivetran](https://www.fivetran.com/blog/fivetran-origins) (YC W13) is the example: the founders built and pitched two different data-analysis tools that both failed, but because they stayed in a fertile idea space and kept talking to the same customers, they eventually stumbled into the tool (a Salesforce-to-Redshift connector) that became the company.

### 3. Three things that make an idea *seem* bad but actually make it good
Most founders shy away from these, which is exactly why they're underexploited:

- **Ideas that are hard to get started.** Paul Graham's essay ["Schlep Blindness"](https://www.paulgraham.com/schlep.html) is the reference here. Thousands of developers knew payment integration was broken before [Stripe](https://stripe.com) existed — but the schlep of getting bank deals and learning card-network internals scared everyone else off, leaving a hundred-billion-dollar opportunity on the table.
- **Ideas in a boring space.** [Gusto](https://gusto.com) (payroll software) is the example — boring enough that it sat unsolved for years despite everyone knowing it sucked. Friedman's point: six to twelve months into any startup, day-to-day work is mostly the same (write code, fix bugs, talk to users) regardless of how exciting the original idea sounded.
- **Ideas with existing competitors.** [Dropbox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropbox) launched as roughly the 20th cloud file-storage company. The crowded market wasn't a red flag — it was evidence real demand existed that nobody had served well. Drew Houston's insight (sync files automatically via OS-level integration, instead of manual browser uploads) was the real differentiator.

### 4. How to come up with startup ideas: notice, don't invent
About 70% of YC's top 100 companies found their idea organically rather than by deliberately sitting down to brainstorm — deliberate brainstorming tends to surface the same tar pit ideas. Three ways to put yourself in a position to notice good ideas over time:
- Become an expert in something valuable (working at a startup is a fast path to this).
- If you're a programmer, build things you find interesting even if they aren't obviously businesses — [Replit](https://www.frederick.ai/blog/amjad-masad-replit) is the example: Amjad Masad's side project wasn't meant to be a startup when he started it.

### 5. Seven recipes for generating startup ideas, ranked by how likely they are to work
1. **Start with what your team is especially good at.** [Resi/Rezi](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/rezi) is the example — the founders had real-estate and debt-financing backgrounds and deliberately searched only within that idea space, which gave them near-instant founder-market fit.
2. **Start with a problem you've personally encountered, ideally one you're unusually positioned to see.** [Vetcove](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/vetcove) — the founders are brothers whose veterinarian father ordered supplies by phone; thousands of vets had the same pain but almost none of them start tech companies, so the idea sat untouched. Friedman's concrete exercise: for every founder, go through every job, internship, and life experience and ask what problems you were in a special position to see.
3. **Think of something you personally wish existed.** [DoorDash](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/doordash-ceo-solving-problems-time-starved-people) — Stanford undergrads who wanted food delivered to their dorm. Flagged as the recipe most likely to lead to a tar pit idea — always ask "is there a structural reason this doesn't already exist?"
4. **Look for something that changed recently.** COVID is the macro example; [Gather Town](https://techcrunch.com/2021/03/11/sequoia-capital-puts-millions-of-dollars-into-gather-a-virtual-hq-platform/) pivoted into virtual hangouts once remote socializing suddenly mattered.
5. **Look for a company that succeeded recently and find a new variant.** [Nuvocargo](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/logistics/our-insights/logistics-disruptors-a-shared-digital-platform-for-us-mexico-logistics) (Flexport for Latin America) — founder Deepak Chhugani picked the idea deliberately for its market size and for having a strong proxy (Flexport) even without deep import/export expertise.
6. **Go talk to people and ask what problems they have.** [AtoB](https://www.atob.com/about-us) (fuel cards for trucking) is the fullest example: the founders picked "software for trucking" as a likely-fertile idea space despite no industry background, then physically drove to truck stops to interview drivers and talked to founders of other trucking-space companies until a mental map of good vs. bad ideas emerged.
7. **Look for a big industry that seems broken.** Plus a bonus recipe: find a co-founder who already has an idea (many people on Startup School's co-founder matching are in exactly this position).

Friedman's closing point: it's often genuinely hard to know if an idea is good from analysis alone — ultimately the only reliable test is to launch it and find out.

---

## Key takeaways
1. Start from a real, specific problem — not from a technology looking for an application.
2. Watch for tar pit ideas: common, tempting, and quietly unsolvable for structural reasons.
3. Evaluate ideas against founder-market fit, market size, problem acuity, competition, recent change, proxies, scalability, and idea-space quality — not vibes.
4. Boring, hard-to-start, and competitive-looking ideas are systematically underrated because other founders avoid them.
5. The best ideas are usually noticed, not invented — expertise and organic exposure beat deliberate brainstorming.
6. If deliberately generating ideas, start from your team's specific expertise before anything else.
