# How Startup Fundraising Works — 7 Myths (YC Startup School)

## Summary

Brad Flora (YC Group Partner; founder of Perfect Audience, acquired 2014; former angel/fund investor in 150+ YC companies) busts seven common myths about startup fundraising. Core message: fundraising is unglamorous and mechanical, and far more accessible today than founders assume — the real barrier is usually "make something people want," not secret pitch tactics or a fancy network.

Source: [How Startup Fundraising Works — YC Startup School](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zBUhQPPS9AY)

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. Myth: fundraising is glamorous
It's not Shark Tank — it's repeated one-on-one coffee chats and Zoom calls, a grind of many meetings. Fresh Paint met 160 investors, got 39 yeses, and it still took 4.5 months to raise $1.6M. ([Fresh Paint's blog post on their raise](https://www.freshpaint.io/blog/anatomy-of-a-seed-round-during-covid-19))

### 2. Myth: you need money before you can start building
Build a small/toy version first, get a few users, then raise — traction creates leverage. Solugen built a desk-sized chemical reactor and was selling $10k/month of hydrogen peroxide to hot tub shops before raising; went on to raise $4M, then $400M+.

### 3. Myth: your startup needs to be impressive to raise money
You need to *convince*, not impress — most great startups sound bad at first (Airbnb, DoorDash, OpenSea). Retool's founder skipped the deck and just demoed the live product; the company later hit a $4B valuation.

### 4. Myth: raising is complicated, slow, and expensive
Only Series A/growth rounds are big, slow, and lawyer-heavy — those are what make the news. Seed rounds are small ($500k–a few million), close in days/weeks, and run on YC's [SAFE](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents) (Simple Agreement for Equity, created 2013): 5 pages, effectively two negotiable terms (valuation cap and discount — nobody uses discounts). Astera Bio raised its first ~$1M via SAFEs from angels before approaching pharma investors, and later raised $150M+.

### 5. Myth: raising money means losing control
SAFEs carry no board seats, no shares until conversion, and no information rights — founders keep full control until a priced round. Zapier raised ~$1M once at YC S12, went fully remote a decade before it was normal, never raised again, and is now a $100M+ revenue business.

### 6. On bootstrapping
Bootstrapping forever "stretches fundraising pain across the whole life of the company" — scary, distracting (consulting detours to make payroll), and rarely produces giant companies. Better to raise once early, then never again if you don't want to.

### 7. Myth: you need a fancy network to raise money
Investors care about traction, not pedigree. Podium's founders (two guys from Utah, no Silicon Valley network) were already making tens of thousands/month selling to tire shops before raising; now $100M+/year, $200M+ raised. Aside: never let a "connector" pitch investors on your behalf — always take the meeting yourself and use them only for the introduction.

### 8. Myth: rejection means it's a bad startup
Rejection is universal and not a signal. The Envision (medical device) founder was rejected 50+ times before her first $25k check; the company later sold for $275M. WhatNot got lukewarm seed interest despite growing revenue during its YC batch; two and a half years later it was worth $3.7B.

### The myth underneath all the myths
The unifying misconception is "fundraising isn't for me." In reality it's mechanical, cheap, and founder-friendly today — the job is just to make something people want and describe it plainly.

---

## Key takeaways

1. Fundraising is a grind of many small meetings, not a high-stakes pitch performance
2. Build something small and get users before raising — traction is the real leverage
3. SAFEs make early fundraising fast, cheap, and founder-favorable (no board seats, no info rights)
4. Bootstrapping forever isn't inherently more "in control" than raising once and being done
5. Network and pedigree matter far less than traction
6. Rejection is the norm even for companies that become worth billions
