# Pick One Idea and Go Deep

## Summary
John, a Y Combinator partner, argues that founders overthink idea selection — there's no way to find the "perfect" idea in the abstract, only through customer contact. His fix: stop searching, commit to one idea, go genuinely deep on it, and use real customer data to tell fast whether it's working, or to surface the better idea hiding underneath.

Source: [Y Combinator — Pick One Idea and Go Deep](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R56RJFZBasQ)

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. The "perfect idea" trap
It's impossible to find the perfect idea in the abstract — you can only figure out what to work on by making contact with reality and getting customer feedback. The related trap, "am I the right founder for this?", is often weaponized against yourself; deep curiosity plus talking to customers can substitute for years of domain experience. [Blake Scholl](https://boomsupersonic.com/company/blake-scholl) went from ad tech at Amazon and Groupon to founding Boom Supersonic, now a billion-dollar company.

### 2. Why working on multiple ideas fails
Juggling several ideas at once produces bad signal — without depth on any one of them, you can't tell if something actually works, so you either prematurely abandon a good idea or keep funding a bad one.

### 3. How to actually go deep
Going deep means burning the other boats: explicitly foreclosing the other options, telling existing customers you've pivoted, and committing with single-minded focus — even changing your company's name and positioning if the new direction demands it.

> "You should become an almost unrecognizable version of yourself."

GovDash pivoted 5+ times, changing its name and positioning each time, before its fifth idea — government-contract-winning software — took off; the founders later [raised a $30M Series B](https://www.govdash.com/blog/press-govdash-raises-30m-series-b-to-help-companies-win-and-manage-government-contracts-with-ai).

### 4. Could you run your customer's business?
Talking to 20 customers isn't the bar. The real test is whether, dropped into the business tomorrow, you'd know how to run it — their daily crises, their biggest problems, what they'd pay to fix them. A related framing: could you teach a class on the problem? Do customer research and product-building in a tight loop (understand → build → deeper understanding → better build), not sequentially — shipping something real produces data that complements the abstract knowledge.

### 5. Build at the edge of what AI can do
A good idea in the AI era sits at the frontier — barely works today but clearly improves as models improve (Paul Graham: "[live in the future, build what's missing](http://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)"). It should also verticalize: sell an outcome, not software. As the cost of producing software trends to zero, durable value shifts to customer trust, licenses, regulatory permission, and outcome ownership. [Corgi Insurance](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/corgi-insurance) (YC S24) acquired an insurance carrier mid-batch to own the full underwriting stack rather than staying a broker or MGA.

### 6. Aim at the most ambitious version
An ambitious idea and a modest one cost roughly the same to pursue, but only the ambitious one builds a moat, attracts top talent, and can rewrite a sector of the economy.

### 7. What happens when the idea fails
Even a failed idea, pursued deeply, leaves you with unambiguous customer data and often a better idea underneath — the real structural problem beneath the surface pain point you started with.

### 8. Walk fast in one direction
Sampling a little of everything produces almost no information. Committing to one direction and walking fast generates far more information per unit of time — and might lead somewhere better than where you started. The real failure mode isn't being wrong; it's never deciding.

---

## Key takeaways
1. Stop looking for the perfect idea — pick one and commit fully.
2. Burn the other boats: single-minded focus, even if it means rebranding.
3. The bar for "deep" is operational fluency in the customer's business, not interview count.
4. In the AI era, target ideas at the model frontier, that verticalize into an outcome, and that are maximally ambitious.
5. Indecision — not being wrong — is the real failure mode.
