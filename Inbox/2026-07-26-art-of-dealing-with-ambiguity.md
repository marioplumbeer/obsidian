# The Art of Dealing with Ambiguity

## Summary
Steve Huynh (18 years at Amazon) argues that with truly ambiguous work — a
vague "investigate this" or "make it scale" — the goal isn't to find a
solution but to properly define the problem. Once the problem space is
framed with clear boundaries, success metrics, and constraints, the fog
lifts and the path forward becomes obvious. He lays out a question-driven
process for getting there.

## Decisions & insights
- **The problem is the solution.** For ambiguous asks, spend your energy on
  problem definition rather than jumping to solutions — once the problem is
  well-framed, the ambiguity itself disappears and a path forward appears.
- **Ambiguity is a high-dimensional problem space.** Each unknown variable is
  another dimension. Questions are the tool for collapsing that space down
  to something tractable, not for finding the answer directly.
- **Five-step process for navigating ambiguity:**
  1. **Question dump** — 10-15 minutes listing every question you can think
     of (what do I know, what does success look like, who are the
     stakeholders, what are the constraints/assumptions/timeline/risks).
  2. **Organize by power** — triage into: answers you already know, answers
     you can find, and answers you don't have. Recurse step 1 on anything
     you don't know. Prioritize questions that fix a dimension or let you
     skip whole areas of investigation.
  3. **Seek answers systematically** — take priority questions to the right
     people, and ask each person who to talk to next. Don't be afraid to
     dig up answers yourself first.
  4. **Iterate and refine** — new questions emerging as you get answers is
     expected and a sign of progress, not a setback.
  5. **Document and share** — go back to the requester with your questions,
     answers, and open unknowns. This "takes people along with you" and
     gives them a starting map instead of a blank page.
- **Worked example**: a team asked to "improve reliability" (maximally
  vague) found that ops, product, and leadership each meant something
  different by "reliability." Interviewing stakeholders turned the vague ask
  into a concrete goal — reduce customer-impacting incidents 80% in six
  months while holding performance steady — which the team could actually
  rally around.
- Reframe: a vague request isn't a frustration, it's an opportunity to
  demonstrate the ability to create clarity from chaos.
