# Steve Jobs: Focus Is About Saying No

## Summary
At Apple's WWDC 1997 closing Q&A — Jobs had just returned to Apple via the NeXT acquisition, first as an advisor and then interim CEO, with the company in its worst shape ever — a developer pushed back on Apple killing OpenDoc, a promising but scattered technology. Jobs's answer reframes what "focus" actually means: not choosing what to build, but ruthlessly choosing what *not* to build, even when it's technically impressive and even when it costs you publicly.

Source: [Focusing is about saying no - Steve Jobs (WWDC '97)](https://youtu.be/H8eP99neOVs)

---

## Decisions & insights

### Focus is subtractive, not additive
Jobs corrects the intuitive framing head-on: "You think about focusing, right? You think, 'well, focusing is saying yes'. No, focusing is about saying 'no'. Focusing is about saying no. And you've got to say 'no, no, no'." Most people equate focus with picking the right thing to pursue; Jobs treats it as an act of continuous refusal — the yes is easy, the no is the actual skill.

### The farm-animals diagnosis of un-focus
Describing pre-return Apple's sprawl of parallel projects: "You look at the farm that's been created with all these different animals going in different directions and it doesn't add up. The total is less than the sum of the parts." Too many initiatives running in uncoordinated directions doesn't just fail to add value — it actively subtracts from the whole, because effort and attention get divided rather than compounded.

### Killing OpenDoc: good tech, wrong fit
On the specific decision that prompted the question: "I was for putting a bullet in the head of OpenDoc. I mean, I think it was great technology but it didn't fit. The rest of the world isn't going to use OpenDoc." The bar for keeping a project isn't "is this good work" — it's "does this fit where the company needs to go, and will the world actually adopt it." Impressive technology that doesn't fit the strategy still has to go.

### Taking the lumps in public
Jobs acknowledges the reputational cost of saying no visibly: "When you say no, you piss off people. And then you go talk to [the press] and they write a shitty article about you... Apple's been taking their share of lumps for the last 6 months in a very unfair way and has been taking them like an adult. And I'm proud of that." Cutting projects publicly generates bad press and angry constituencies; he treats absorbing that cost as a sign of maturity, not a failure of communications.

### The payoff: multiplication, not addition
His closing line reframes the entire trade: "Focusing is about saying no. And the result of that focus is going to be some really great products where the total is much greater than the sum of the parts." The same portfolio of effort, concentrated instead of scattered, doesn't just avoid the farm-animals problem — it compounds into something categorically better than any of the abandoned parts would have produced alone.

---

## Key takeaways
1. Focus is measured by what you kill, not by what you greenlight — the "no" list is the real strategy document.
2. "Good technology" is not sufficient justification to keep a project; fit with strategy and real-world adoption are the actual bar.
3. Saying no in public costs reputation in the short term (bad press, upset constituencies) — that cost is often worth paying and shouldn't be avoided by staying vague or non-committal.
4. Scattered effort across many initiatives can make the whole company *less* than the sum of its parts; concentrated effort across fewer initiatives can make it *more*.
