# Elizabeth Stone (Netflix CPTO) — Systems Thinking in the AI Era

## Summary

Elizabeth Stone, Netflix's Chief Product and Technology Officer, returns to Lenny's Podcast two and a half years after her first appearance to talk about how GenAI has scrambled — and is reshaping — product, engineering, and design roles at Netflix. Her core argument: functional craft (engineering, design, data science) isn't disappearing, but the premium skill across every function is now systems thinking, because AI lets anyone prototype anything and someone still has to decide what's worth building and how it should scale. She also unpacks Netflix's "excellence as an operating system" culture (talent density, the keeper's test, resistance to process) and why it's aging well in a world where frontier AI labs are re-discovering the same principles.

Source: [Why Netflix is betting on systems thinkers—not specialists—in the AI era | Elizabeth Stone (CPTO)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t0GiTyz4syY) — Lenny's Podcast

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. AI causes a "storming before forming" phase of role confusion
Any transformative new technology triggers a storming phase before a forming phase, and Netflix is in the middle of it now. PMs shipping code, designers writing PRDs, and engineers doing product work is creating real confusion about "what is my job anymore" — but Stone doesn't think the fix is to restrict AI use. It's to be thoughtful about capturing the benefit (faster prototyping, faster hypothesis-testing) while installing guardrails: clarity on source-of-truth data, review processes before big changes, and constant reiteration that a human is still accountable for the outcome even when an agent wrote the code.

> "I don't think that means we should put AI back into the box and say let's not use it... but I do think it means we have to be much more thoughtful about how do we get the benefits while reducing the costs."

### 2. Roles have blurred, but comparative advantage hasn't disappeared
PMs, designers, and data scientists can now get much further into the product development lifecycle before engineering has to unblock them — prototyping, coding, and testing hypotheses faster than two years ago. AI is also powerful at distilling Netflix's decades of institutional experiments and consumer insight into a usable head start. But the comparative strengths of each discipline still hold: data scientists own "can we trust this data," PMs own "have we framed the right problem," and engineers own "how does this scale and what breaks."

### 3. Craft excellence remains scarce, and functions aren't going away
Even with AI blurring the lines of who does what, Stone doesn't see specialized disciplines disappearing anytime soon. Someone still has to judge what "good" looks like, and that judgment is what's scarce — not the raw ability to generate a prototype.

> "I still find great engineering to be scarce, great data science to be scarce, great creativity to be scarce."

### 4. Netflix is hiring systems thinkers, not local experts
The skill in shortest supply now is people who can look across every business domain and abstract it into reusable building blocks. In infrastructure, that means moving away from "each team builds its own stack" toward paved paths and shared source-of-truth data, because agents operating across many systems need common rails to be safe and fast. In design, it means fewer people optimizing one feature and more people building design systems and templates so non-designers can ship coherent, on-brand experiences instead of "shipping Frankensteins." The shift is a mindset change as much as a skill change — Netflix is explicitly hiring for comfort with ambiguity and curiosity, not just technical range.

### 5. Systems thinking matters more because velocity and agent scaffolding demand it
Platforms already gave teams leverage; AI raises the stakes because more people (and agents) are now doing more different kinds of work, which multiplies questions about access, identity, security, and quality. An organization of thousands can no longer rely on tribal knowledge — the "ask the one person who knows" model doesn't scale. Netflix's answer is to encode "what good looks like" into paved paths and scaffolding rather than have every builder rediscover it, especially as more of the actual work gets done by agents instead of only humans.

### 6. The design process isn't dead — it just runs faster
Responding directly to [Jenny Wen's "design process is dead" thesis](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-design-process-is-dead) from a separate Lenny's Podcast episode, Stone pushes back for large-scale consumer products: designers now have more tools and can iterate faster, but deep design thinking is still core to what makes Netflix's product feel simple despite immense underlying complexity. The work looks different: less time on ceremony, same commitment to the mindset.

> "I think it would be a mistake to say design and deep design expertise and thinking gets squeezed out just because we can write code faster."

### 7. Narrow specialization is trending down — adaptability is trending up
Compared to five or ten years ago, Netflix needs fewer narrow specialists (e.g., "I'm only a front-end engineer") and more people who can move across the stack or across functions, because AI has made it easier for a specialist to pick up adjacent tools. Deep subject-matter expertise (payments, ads marketplace, a specific studio production tool) is still valuable, but only paired with a willingness to keep asking whether the old tool or approach is still the right one.

### 8. A practical trick for building systems-thinking muscle
Stone's tactic: for whatever problem you're handed, "step out one click" and ask what you're assuming about the broader space before you solve it — without spending so long questioning that you stall forward progress. A complementary lens: think about the problem the way your manager would, across finance, content, and the rest of the business, not just your one team's KPI. That's also, not incidentally, good career advice — making your manager's job easier tends to pay off.

> "Each problem you're trying to solve, step out one click. What am I assuming is true about the broader space?"

### 9. AI fluency is an overlay across career ladders, not a rewritten rubric
Rather than redefining every level's expectations for AI, Netflix layered an aspiration for "AI fluency" across all roles and levels — an experimentation mindset, judgment about where AI is and isn't useful, open-mindedness to try new things. It applies at the most senior levels too (Stone includes herself, despite not writing code day-to-day) and now shows up in hiring: candidates are evaluated on how they think about technology and change, and coding interviews explicitly allow AI tool use since that's part of the real job now.

### 10. AI's biggest wins at Netflix go well beyond coding
Two under-discussed use cases: (1) distilling decades of experiment history and consumer research into fast, actionable insight — the use case Stone personally relies on most; and (2) content production and creation, where GenAI is a step-function on top of ML/AI Netflix has used for years (promotional assets at scale, localization/dubs). The clearest example: Netflix's acquisition of [InterPositive](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/netflix-paid-587-million-ben-affleck-ai-interpositive-1236815111/), the AI filmmaking company co-founded by Ben Affleck, whose tools let filmmakers relight, reframe, reshoot, and change dialogue after a shoot — always at the filmmaker's direction, not as a replacement for one.

### 11. Netflix's AI history predates the GenAI moment by two decades
The Netflix Prize — a widely publicized contest to improve Netflix's recommendation algorithm — is Stone's go-to reminder that AI/ML has been central to personalization since long before "AI" was the popular label for it (it used to be called machine learning, deliberately kept separate from "AI" as a term). That history gives Netflix a head start on today's harder version of the same problem: personalizing an ever-broadening catalog (film, TV, games, live, podcasts) that makes discovery progressively more difficult.

### 12. "Excellence as an operating system" is the real point of Netflix's culture
Traits from Netflix's original culture deck — high agency, autonomy, talent density, paying top of market, bottom-up decision-making — aren't values for their own sake. They're mechanisms for reaching excellence: give people real accountability and trust their judgment, and you get both better outcomes and more intrinsic motivation. Stone notes, half-joking, that frontier AI labs are now re-arriving at operating principles Netflix has run on from the start.

> "The goal of all those cultural elements wasn't the end goal in themselves... it was instead a very strongly held opinion that you get to excellence by giving people a lot of agency and accountability."

### 13. The pillars: talent density, real risk tolerance, and resisting process
Talent density is non-negotiable — without it, an organization can't trust decision-making at every level. Beyond that, leaders have to be comfortable letting people make decisions the leader might have made differently, as long as it's not catastrophic, and treat mistakes as learning rather than reasons to add process. Stone's clearest example is Netflix's foray into live events: high risk, imperfect execution, fast learning — and a source of real pride rather than embarrassment. Her broader observation: every time Netflix has responded to a hard problem (planning, leveling, comp) by adding more process, it got more time spent without better outcomes.

> "It's a resistance to do the thing that a lot of bigger companies would do, and to feel comfortable in that discomfort very often."

### 14. The keeper's test is mostly a positive, ongoing conversation
Contrary to its reputation as an exit-conversation tool, the keeper's test is invoked constantly as a check-in — a direct report asking "how am I doing on your keeper test," to which Stone's answer is usually "I would fight so hard to keep you," followed by concrete feedback on strengths and growth areas. The harder version (telling someone they're not passing) still exists, but the test's real value is forcing consistent, honest feedback instead of avoiding the conversation — in both directions.

### 15. Attracting talent against frontier AI labs means leaning into the entertainment application, not the model race
Netflix isn't losing the talent war so much as competing for a different persona than frontier labs attract: people who love the application of technology to entertainment and consumer products at global scale, not foundational model research for its own sake. Stone is explicit that this is a different type of excitement, not a lesser one, and Netflix's pitch is built around that distinction rather than trying to out-compete labs on compensation or compute.

### 16. Junior hiring continues, but craft mastery is still the bar
Netflix still runs intern and new-grad programs (the latter added only a few years ago) because younger hires bring native fluency with new tools and current consumer behavior. But easier tooling doesn't reduce the responsibility a junior engineer or designer carries for the quality of what they ship — mentorship now has to teach both "how to use these tools" and "what good still looks like," since craft excellence remains scarce. Stone expects the teaching to run in both directions: junior talent will also be teaching senior people new things.

### 17. Understanding systems still matters even if you don't write every line
Stone draws a line between writing code in a specific language and understanding how the resulting system actually works — the latter doesn't go away, because when something breaks (and Netflix's culture deliberately takes risks and expects fast recovery), someone has to know why and how to fix it. She's candid that this is currently the hardest part of the transition: code generated by agents can be difficult to follow, which is "unsettling," but she expects engineering to develop new fluency and rationalization tools for operating in that mode, the same way engineering (and filmmaking) has reinvented itself every decade for a century.

### 18. The future of entertainment is multi-format, not just film and TV
Netflix's expansion into games, live events, and podcasts reflects a bet that entertainment stops being one thing — consumers expect a seamless journey across formats and moments of the day (a podcast episode leading to a show leading to a mobile game). The harder problem this creates is discovery and engagement across a much more fragmented catalog, which Stone sees as squarely Netflix's problem to solve given its history at the intersection of entertainment and technology.

### 19. On AI in Hollywood, Netflix's stance is creator enablement, not prescription
Netflix works across the full spectrum of creator sentiment on AI — from filmmakers who want none of it to those actively exploring what GenAI makes newly possible — because entertainment itself isn't going to be one format going forward. Stone doesn't see a future where storytelling loses its human center; she frames storytelling as inseparable from humanity, and expects AI to materially shape *how* stories look and feel without displacing who tells them.

> "I have a hard time picturing entertainment that doesn't have humans at the heart of it."

---

## Key takeaways
1. AI didn't eliminate functional expertise — it raised the value of systems thinking on top of it.
2. Netflix is deliberately hiring for people who abstract across domains and build paved paths, not local specialists.
3. "Step out one click" — question what you're assuming about the broader problem before you solve the narrow one.
4. AI fluency is layered across every level as a mindset, not rewritten per-level as a rubric.
5. Netflix's culture (talent density, real risk tolerance, resisting process) is the same operating system frontier AI labs are now converging on.
6. Craft mastery — knowing what good looks like and being accountable for it — stays scarce and stays valuable, even as tools make output easier to generate.
