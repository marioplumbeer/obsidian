# How to Control Your Thoughts (You Can't — Here's What You Can Do Instead)

## Summary
Dr. Alok Kanojia ("Dr. K"), a Harvard-trained psychiatrist and co-founder of [Healthy Gamer](https://www.healthygamer.gg/dr-alok-kanojia), argues against a claim he hears from almost every highly successful person he studies: that you can control your thoughts. His clinical position is that you can't — intrusive thoughts, spirals, and obsessions prove that — but high performers have still learned three real levers: shifting attention, regulating emotion, and controlling the inputs (environment) that generate their thoughts in the first place.

Source: [Why Staying Positive Doesn't Work (HealthyGamerGG)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l7BaFufR23E)

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. The paradox: successful people insist they control their thoughts — Dr. K says they can't
Dr. K opens with a disagreement he has with nearly every highly successful person across industries, including a clip of podcast host [Lewis Howes](https://lewishowes.com/sogpodcast/) asserting people can choose what they think. Dr. K's clinical position, built from years of treating patients as they go from moderately to highly successful, is that this is technically false — but the disagreement itself is the interesting part, because it reveals what these people are *actually* doing instead.

### 2. Intrusive thoughts prove you can't just command a thought away
The clearest evidence: OCD is, by definition, an illness where obsessions intrude and can't be turned off by will. Dr. K also describes a client who exited a company at 42 and then became severely depressed — flooded with negative thoughts despite trying affirmations and telling himself not to think that way. You cannot instruct your own mind the way you'd give it an order ("tomorrow I will think positively") and have it comply.

### 3. You can't stop a thought — but you can shift your attention away from it
Using a clip where Howes admits he can't stop a thought from arising but *can* "shift the thinking," Dr. K frames this as the first real skill: you don't control which channel is broadcasting, only which channel you choose to watch. Left unmanaged, one thought chains into the next — a breakup becomes "I've been dumped" → "I'm unlovable" → "I'll be alone forever" — a thought spiral that compounds for hours and erodes self-esteem. High performers interrupt the chain by redirecting attention rather than arguing with the first thought. He offers a concrete exercise: close your eyes and focus entirely on the tip of your nose for a few seconds — attention shifts, and the prior thought stream stops.

### 4. Your emotions generate your thoughts — so regulate the emotion, not the thought
Thinking isn't separate from emotion; anger, shame, and regret act like "factories" that keep producing negative thoughts as long as they're active — which is the premise behind [cognitive behavioral therapy](https://beckinstitute.org/about/understanding-cbt/): manage the emotion and the thinking changes with it. Dr. K draws a distinction between *suppressing* an emotion (scrolling social media, watching distracting videos — which just delays the emotion and adds regret later) and *regulating* it (breathing techniques, which physiologically shift emotional state and are fast enough to teach even young children).

### 5. Arguing with yourself rarely works — controlling your inputs does
Trying to logically talk yourself out of a feeling ("I don't have low self-esteem, here's my pro/con list") mostly fails as long as an emotion is still fueling the thought — he's seen esports athletes spend hours a day arguing with themselves with no performance gain. What actually works is controlling the sensory inputs that generate thoughts in the first place — the same principle propaganda and advertising exploit through repeated sensory bombardment. High performers curate their environment deliberately (nutrition, coaching, peers, recovery routines) instead of spending hours comparing themselves to competitors.

### 6. High performers treat a setback as an obstacle to solve; low performers treat it as a verdict
Dr. K's "garden" analogy: you can't stop weeds from growing, only weed daily — plant good inputs, remove negative thoughts as they surface. The difference between high and low performers isn't the presence of negative thoughts, it's the response to them: "this is an obstacle, how do I overcome it" versus "this is an obstacle, therefore I can't." He notes this compounding process takes real time — his coaching program sees real change appear around 4–8 weeks in, but sees its best outcomes closer to 20 weeks, which mirrors how long it takes to bring an overgrown garden under control.

> "This is an obstacle. How do I overcome it? That is the difference between a high performer and a low performer's thought."

The single weed he tells everyone to pull first: black-and-white thinking. Almost nothing is actually binary, and he notes the intensity of black-and-white thinking clinically correlates with the severity of mental illness (a diagnostic feature of borderline personality disorder, and present in both psychotic depression and mania). The fix isn't forcing the opposite extreme — it's shifting toward gray.

---

## Key takeaways
1. You can't directly control which thoughts arise — intrusive thoughts and thought spirals prove that.
2. You can shift your attention away from an unproductive thought (the "channel" you choose to watch).
3. Regulate the emotion driving the thought (breathing) rather than suppressing it (distraction) or arguing with it (logic).
4. Curate your environment/inputs — they shape your thinking more than willpower does.
5. Treat setbacks as solvable obstacles, not verdicts, and expect real change to take weeks, not days.
6. Watch for black-and-white thinking — it's the first thing to weed out.
