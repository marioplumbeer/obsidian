# J.L. Collins — The Simple Path to Wealth

## Summary

[J.L. Collins](https://jlcollinsnh.com/), author of *The Simple Path to Wealth*, joins [Andrew Giancola](https://mastermoney.co/) on the Personal Finance Podcast to explain why simplicity beats complexity in investing: avoid debt, live on less than you earn, and put the surplus into low-cost total-market index funds. The book grew out of letters he wrote teaching his daughter about money — she listened eventually, and retired financially independent at 33.

Source: [The Simple Path to Wealth (With J.L. Collins) — The Personal Finance Podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-02BNewBfbo)

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. Smart people overcomplicate money because simplicity feels suspicious

In most of life, more effort produces better results, so smart people assume investing must work the same way. It doesn't — decades of research show the less you tinker, the better your results. Collins credits [Charlie Munger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Munger)'s line that you never want to get in the way of compounding, and [Jack Bogle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Bogle)'s "don't just do something, stand there" for the same idea: set up an automated plan in low-cost index funds, then leave it alone.

### 2. A losing bet early is a lucky bet

Collins bought his first stock (Warren Buffett–style value picks) in his youth and lost badly; Andrew's own first trade was a penny stock at 17 that wiped out overnight. Collins' point: the *outcome* doesn't validate the *decision* — a concept he ties to [Annie Duke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Duke)'s book *Thinking in Bets*. If the penny stock had skyrocketed instead, that would have been the worse outcome — it teaches false confidence, leading to bigger and bigger bets until one implodes. Money earned quickly and easily (speculation, lottery wins, early fame) is rarely "sticky" — it gets spent or lost. Money that's hard-won gets kept.

### 3. The one-sentence philosophy: avoid debt, live on less than you earn, buy your freedom with the surplus

Collins graduated in 1972 into stagflation, took two years to land a professional job at $10K/year (~$50K today), and arbitrarily committed to saving/investing 50% of it — because financial independence was the single thing he most wanted to buy with his money. As income grew, both the savings rate and the lifestyle could grow together. The harder case isn't the young — it's people who've already built a lifestyle that consumes every dollar (or requires borrowing); unwinding that is much harder than never building it.

### 4. Low-cost index funds win because of cost compounding and "self-cleansing"

Two reasons: (1) a 1% fee looks small but compounds into a huge drag over decades, and (2) a total-market or S&P 500 index owns tomorrow's winners automatically and lets today's losers fade out on their own — a process Collins calls self-cleansing. That also removes the hardest question in stock-picking: not just what to buy, but when to sell. Roughly 90% of professional fund managers fail to beat the S&P 500 in any given year, and the 10% who do beat it aren't consistently the same firms year over year.

> "It's kind of like saying, 'You know what? I could take some lessons and get in the ring with Mike Tyson.'"

### 5. VTSAX over the S&P 500 — a matter of taste, not performance

VTSAX (Vanguard's [Total Stock Market Index Fund](https://investor.vanguard.com/investment-products/mutual-funds/profile/vtsax)) tracks almost identically to the S&P 500 because both are cap-weighted and dominated by the same largest companies. Collins' preference for VTSAX's ~3,600 holdings over the S&P's 500 is "a little bit of extra spice" from the added small/mid-cap exposure — not a meaningful performance edge. If a 401(k) only offers an S&P 500 fund, that's still "golden" — Jack Bogle himself held the S&P 500 fund for life. During the accumulation phase, Collins holds no bonds: regular paycheck contributions already smooth the ride by buying more shares when prices dip.

### 6. Young investors should root for a crash

The best thing that can happen to someone starting out is a market crash, because it means buying shares cheap. Collins points to the 2000s "lost decade" (dot-com crash, then 2008) — a terrible decade to be *invested*, but a magnificent one to have *started* investing in, since it was followed by a ~15–20 year bull market from March 2009 onward. Pullbacks are a normal, recurring feature (expect at least one every ~10 years across a career), not a signal to panic-sell.

### 7. It's never too late — the FI journey is 10–15 years regardless of starting age

If you're serious and willing to commit roughly half your income, financial independence takes 10–15 years from zero, no matter your starting age. Collins references [Leo Burnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Burnett)'s line — "if you reach for a star, you might not get one, but you won't come up with a handful of mud either" — even partial progress toward FI leaves you meaningfully better off than not trying.

### 8. Spending isn't deprivation if it buys what you actually value

The common objection to aggressive saving is that it feels like deprivation. Collins reframes it: he was still spending money — just on the thing he wanted most (freedom), calling it his "few money." If FI isn't the thing you want most, that's fine too — but at least you know the option existed. The "skill of spending" is figuring out what you truly value, funding that fully, and cutting everything else — including areas people don't examine (a cluttered garage, stuff nobody uses).

### 9. The 4% rule tells you what "enough" actually is

Collins credits [Bill Bengen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bengen)'s original research (later reinforced by the [Trinity study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_study)) for the rule: withdraw ~4% of a portfolio annually, adjusted for inflation, and it should survive a 30-year retirement even in historically bad markets. Two ways to use it: multiply your desired annual spend by 25 to get your FI number (e.g. $100K/year → $2.5M), or take 4% of what you already have to see what it funds. FI isn't a fixed dollar figure — it's a formula relating spend to savings. Once you hit your number, more saving just makes an already-bought thing bigger; it doesn't buy more freedom.

### 10. Once you've bought your freedom, you don't have to keep buying it

Collins compares this to buying a car: once you own it, you don't need to buy another one next month. People who are excellent at the accumulation phase often struggle to "flip the switch" and start spending, over-accumulating instead. His own resolution: money he doesn't need for something meaningful should go toward the things he actually cares about (he flies first class despite calling it a bad value proposition, because it makes flying "slightly less terrible") — or toward helping other people, since spending money is itself a way of putting it into the hands of people who need it.

### 11. Buying time back is one of the best trades there is

For years Collins' guest Andrew mowed his own lawn to avoid a $75/month fee, until his first child was born and he realized the time-value of that money. Collins adds: money spent on services doesn't just buy time back — it moves that money to people who'll make good use of it. His broader take on spending and happiness: money removes *unhappiness* caused by scarcity (rent, food, car repairs), but past a comfortable baseline, spending itself is a weak lever for happiness — anticipation of a purchase (planning a trip, researching an item) often outweighs the thing itself once acquired. He tells a story about Warren Buffett driving the same Cadillac for over a decade, reportedly because he didn't want to waste half a day buying a new one.

### 12. What Collins would emphasize now, and the biggest mistake in your 30s

Asked what he'd tell someone starting today with high student debt, high housing costs, and a shakier job market: the same principles apply — today's environment is easier than the stagflation of the 1970s he came up in, except for student debt, which he considers avoidable (community college for two years, working while studying, avoiding loans altogether). The single biggest money mistake in your early 30s, in his view, is lifestyle inflation the moment income rises — taking on a nicer apartment or car payment instead of directing new income toward freedom.

### 13. Teaching kids: three jars, and living the example

Collins uses a "give / save / spend" jar system with his kids (roughly 50% to save, the rest split), but stresses that principles taught by lecture don't stick — kids model what their parents actually do with money, not what they're told. His own daughter tuned him out as a teenager because he pushed the lessons too hard too early, then came around later by watching how her parents actually lived — and is now financially independent and retired at 33 herself.

## Key takeaways
1. Simplicity beats complexity in investing — set up an automated plan in low-cost index funds and leave it alone.
2. An early loss teaches more cheaply than an early win; don't confuse luck with skill.
3. The whole philosophy in one sentence: avoid debt, live on less than you earn, invest the surplus to buy freedom.
4. Low-cost total-market index funds win on cost and on not having to guess winners, losers, or sell timing.
5. It's never too late to start — the FI timeline is 10–15 years regardless of age, given serious commitment.
6. Financial independence is a formula (annual spend × 25), not a fixed number — and once you hit it, you don't have to keep "buying" it.
7. Spend deliberately on what you actually value; past a comfortable baseline, spending is a weak lever for happiness.
8. Kids learn from what parents do with money, not what they're told to do with it.
