# David Ayer — Writing Training Day With Real Gang Consultants

## Summary

Short clip of screenwriter David Ayer describing how *Training Day* evolved from a softer, more conventional buddy-cop draft into the harder film that got made — and how director Antoine Fuqua's involvement, plus direct access to real gang members and gang-unit cops during development, reshaped the script's tone and dialogue.

Source: [YouTube Short](https://youtube.com/shorts/nApxNz_US1g)

---

## Decisions & insights

### 1. The early draft was softer than the finished film

Ayer describes the original script as closer in tone to *Lethal Weapon* than to *Training Day* — a more ordinary, low-key version of the dirty-cop character. The tone hardened once Antoine Fuqua came on board and brought in real gang culture: actual Crips and Bloods, plus cops who'd worked gang units, as consultants during development.

### 2. "You have to be a gangster to deal with gangsters"

Gang-unit cops told Ayer that survival in that world comes down to constant uncertainty about who is informing on whom — nobody can be sure who's talking. He built that dynamic directly into the film: a corrupt cop can bluff his way through a room of gang members just by implying he has informants inside, planting doubt without proof.

### 3. Slang came from consultants, not invention

Rather than inventing generic "gangster talk," Ayer pulled specific terminology straight from the people he was interviewing:
- A former leader of the Athens Park Bloods explained real prison slang for a disciplinary housing term Ayer had initially misheard as something unrelated.
- Consultants gave him the actual street term used for smoking PCP, tied to its physical effect on the user.

His method: keep asking practitioners what they actually call something, then write the scene around their literal answer instead of an approximation.

---

## Takeaway

The film's authenticity came from treating consultants as co-authors of texture — sourcing exact phrases, unwritten survival rules, and procedural details (like disciplinary housing) from people who had lived them, rather than writing gang culture from the outside.
