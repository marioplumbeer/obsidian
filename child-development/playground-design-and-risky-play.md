# Playground Design and the Case for Risky Play

## Summary

TED-Ed explainer (*Why kids need to take more risks*, directed by AIM Creative Studios with the LEGO Group and LEGO Foundation) on how playground design shapes child development. Traces the idea back to 1930s "junk playgrounds" and argues that today's standardized swings-and-slides playgrounds under-deliver on the developmental benefits of free, open-ended, and moderately risky play.

Source: [TED-Ed — Why kids need to take more risks](https://youtu.be/kRAl4Xgs_NU)

---

## Key insights

### 1. Origin story: the junk playground

In the 1930s, Danish landscape architect Carl Theodor Sørensen noticed neighborhood kids preferred playing in abandoned building sites over formal playgrounds — climbing exposed beams, scavenging materials, building things themselves. He turned an old housing estate into a dedicated "junk playground," which spread across Europe, including sites built out of derelict lots and old WWI bomb sites.

### 2. Why play matters developmentally

Play builds problem-solving (through inventing games and rules), motor control (moving through varied terrain), and social/language skills (playing with others). These benefits emerge naturally in most play environments, but three factors maximize them:
- **Freedom** — kids need real agency to explore and shape their environment without adult interference.
- **Novelty** — new, unpredictable elements keep play engaging.
- **Time** — enough of it to actually follow a plan through.

### 3. The affordance problem with standard playgrounds

Play researchers use "affordance" to describe how open-ended an object's design is. A sandbox has high affordance (many possible uses); a slide has low affordance (one intended use). Standard playground equipment — swings, slides, climbing frames — tends to be low-affordance and low-novelty, which caps how much developmental benefit kids get from it even when they get creative with it.

### 4. Modern adventure playgrounds revive the junk-playground model

Designers are building large, high-affordance, free-play spaces modeled on the old junk playgrounds:
- **Kolle 37** (Berlin) — scrap materials and real building tools kids can use.
- **Hanegi Playpark** (Tokyo) — a muddy park where kids shape their own water slides, sinkholes, and splash pools out of the landscape.
- **Glamis Adventure Playground** (London) — kids use fire pits under the supervision of dedicated "play worker" staff.

### 5. The research case for risky play

Researcher Ellen Sandseter's work argues that letting kids experiment with speed, height, and even getting lost builds judgment, confidence, and self-management of risk. Counterintuitively, one study found more serious injuries at traditional playgrounds than adventure playgrounds — possibly because adventure play makes kids more attentive to their own surroundings. Risky play is also linked to better long-term anxiety management, since small, manageable doses of uncertainty build tolerance for an unpredictable world. Nature-based playgrounds (creeks, caves, trees) offer a related version of this, combining risk-taking with time outdoors.

### 6. Bringing freedom and novelty into conventional designs

Not every solution requires a full adventure playground. Examples of hybrid approaches: a 30-person mega-swing in a Philadelphia park, and Omaha's Riverfront Playground, which uses a warping, multi-level decking "spine" to create tunnels and rope-climb areas within a more traditional park format.

---

## Takeaway

No single playground type is "the answer" — the goal is a mix of play environments that offer real agency, novelty, and a measured dose of risk, so kids can fill in the possibilities themselves rather than being handed a fixed script for how to play.
