# Steve Jobs on Failure

## Summary
In a 1994 interview with the Santa Clara Valley Historical Association (conducted by John McLaughlin), Steve Jobs — then still at NeXT, two years before his return to Apple — lays out a simple filter for who actually gets things done versus who just dreams about them: willingness to ask for help, and willingness to fail. He illustrates it with the story of cold-calling Bill Hewlett at age 12 to ask for spare parts, and generalizes it into a broader philosophy about shaping rather than passively living in the world you're given.

Source: [Steve Jobs on Failure](https://youtu.be/zkTf0LmDqKI)

---

## Decisions & insights

### Most people never ask
Jobs's core claim: "I've never found anybody that didn't want to help me if I asked them for help." The gap between people who accomplish things and people who don't isn't raw ability — it's that most people never pick up the phone and ask.

> "Most people never pick up the phone and call. Most people never ask. And that's what separates, sometimes, the people that do things from the people that just dream about them."

### The Bill Hewlett story
At 12, Jobs looked up Bill Hewlett (of Hewlett-Packard) in the phone book, called him directly, and asked for spare parts to build a frequency counter. Hewlett not only gave him the parts — he gave Jobs a summer job at HP. The point isn't the specific ask; it's that the ask itself was nearly free and the downside of a "no" was negligible compared to the upside.

### Failure is the toll, not the exception
Asking and acting both carry real risk of rejection or failure, and Jobs treats that as simply the price of admission: "You gotta act. You gotta be willing to fail. You gotta be willing to crash and burn... If you're afraid you'll fail, you won't get very far." Fear of failure isn't a rational reason to hold back — it's the mechanism that keeps most people from ever finding out what they could do.

### Life is something you shape, not something you live in
Jobs frames this as a bigger worldview shift: "shake off this erroneous notion that life is there and you're just gonna live in it, versus embrace it, change it, improve it, make your mark upon it." Once you realize you can "poke life and actually something will pop out the other side," the asymmetry becomes obvious — most things you'd want to change have never really been tried by the person complaining about them.

---

## Key takeaways
1. The cost of asking is usually near-zero; the cost of not asking is never finding out. Treat "no" as the expected, affordable outcome of an ask, not a reason to avoid making it.
2. Willingness to fail isn't optional for people who "do things" — it's the entry fee, not a risk to be engineered away.
3. Direct, unglamorous action (a phone call, a cold ask) often outperforms waiting for the right introduction or credential.
4. Treating the world as moldable rather than fixed reframes obstacles as things nobody has bothered to push on yet, not immovable constraints.
