# The BCG & McKinsey Problem-Solving Process

## Summary

A structured, 7-step, hypothesis-led method that top consulting firms use to tackle complex business problems with incomplete data — market entry, cost reduction, M&A. The core idea: form a hypothesis about the answer early, then design analysis to test it, rather than boiling the ocean on data collection first.

Source: [The BCG and McKinsey problem solving process — A step-by-step guide](https://slideworks.io/resources/mckinsey-problem-solving-process) · Kasper Vardrup (ex-Bain) & Mats Stigzelius (ex-McKinsey), Slideworks

---

## The 7 steps

### 1. Define the problem
Pin down the exact question before doing anything else, using a **Problem Statement Worksheet**: main question (phrased SMART — Specific, Measurable, Action-oriented, Relevant, Time-bound), context, success criteria, scope/constraints, key stakeholders, and information sources.

Example SMART question: *"How can Airline Inc. reduce operating costs by $400 million through more efficient and effective operations before 2027?"*

### 2. Structure the problem
Break the main question into a **hypothesis tree** — a set of testable hypotheses about the answer. A good hypothesis is testable, debatable (not a stated fact), significant to the outcome, and leads to a specific action.

Example, from the Airline Inc. case: fleet optimization (~$150M), operational efficiency (~$120M), procurement (~$80M), technology/automation (~$50M).

If there isn't enough information to hypothesize upfront, build an **issue tree** instead — decompose the problem into the key questions that need answering. Either tree must be **MECE** (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive).

### 3. Prioritize issues
Filter the tree with a **2x2 matrix**, most commonly Impact vs. Ease of Implementation (other useful axes: urgency, strategic fit, capability fit). This step is meant to cut a large chunk of the tree before real analysis work starts.

### 4. Develop the issue analysis / work plan
Turn the prioritized issues into a concrete plan: specific tasks with defined outcomes, the analyses and data sources each needs, timing, and who owns what — sequenced against milestones and available resources.

### 5. Conduct analyses
The most time-intensive step. Two operating principles: focus on solutions, not analysis for its own sake — actionable insight beats perfect data — and apply the **80/20 rule** to concentrate effort where it yields most of the answer. Quantitative data is treated as paramount.

### 6. Synthesize findings
Distill the analysis into one governing insight, using the **Pyramid Principle** ("every synthesis should communicate one main idea") structured as **Situation → Complication → Resolution**:

- *Situation*: current context (e.g. industry-wide cost pressure from fuel, competition, pandemic recovery)
- *Complication*: the problem this creates (Airline Inc.'s costs exceed benchmarks, threatening profitability)
- *Resolution*: the proposed fix ($400M cost reduction across fleet, operations, procurement, technology)

Synthesis isn't a final step — it should happen continuously through the project, not just at the end.

### 7. Develop recommendations
Convert the resolution into an implementable action plan: sequenced steps with timelines, clear ownership, and named success factors/obstacles. Before finalizing, check: do stakeholders understand what's changing and why; are leaders committed to the behavioral change; are supporting systems (incentives, evaluations) in place; does the team have the skills and confidence to execute?

## Process characteristics

- **Iterative, not linear** — despite the numbered steps, real problem-solving loops: form early hypotheses, test, refine, repeat.
- **Collaborative** — kept open with team members and stakeholders; the solution is co-created, not delivered.
- **Evidence-based** — leans on quantitative backing throughout, avoiding conclusions the data doesn't support.

## Key takeaways
1. Hypothesize first, then design analysis to test the hypothesis — don't start from open-ended data gathering.
2. MECE structuring (hypothesis or issue tree) and a 2x2 impact/ease filter keep the problem scoped to what's worth analyzing.
3. The 80/20 rule applies to the analysis phase itself: aim for the fraction of the work that yields most of the answer.
4. Synthesize continuously via Situation-Complication-Resolution, not just once at the end.
5. A recommendation isn't done until ownership, timeline, and organizational readiness (systems, skills, buy-in) are addressed.
